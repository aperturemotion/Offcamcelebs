An American company bought Afterpay for twenty-nine billion dollars.
But when the deal closed, its own filing put the price at thirteen point eight.
So which number is right?
Let us explain.
In August twenty twenty-one, Jack Dorsey's company, Square, agreed to buy Afterpay.
But Square didn't pay in cash.
It paid in its own shares.
For every Afterpay share, you got point three seven five of a Square share.
At the time, each Square share was worth two hundred and forty-seven dollars.
Multiply it out, and you get twenty-nine billion.
But here's the catch.
The deal took six months to close.
By then, Square had renamed itself Block, and its share price had roughly halved.
Same number of shares. About half the price.
So when the deal closed, Block told the S.E.C. what those shares were worth: thirteen point eight billion.
To be fair, twenty-nine billion was real on day one, and Afterpay's shareholders got the shares they were promised.
But the price tag everyone remembers was never the price paid.
Our rating: inflated.

- "got the shares they were promised": the fixed 0.375 ratio was delivered (113,617,352 shares issued); nothing says who kept them.
- No one "lost" money, no one misled anyone, no current share price, the two banned words absent, the rating is on the $29 billion figure as the price paid.
